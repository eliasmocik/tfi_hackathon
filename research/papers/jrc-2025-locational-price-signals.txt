Abstract
As Europe strives to become the first climate neutral continent, grid congestion is becoming an
increasingly urgent issue, with the potential to lead to large-scale curtailment of renewables and
undermine the system’s security. Accordingly, the Mission Letter to Commissioner for Energy and
Affordable Housing Dan Jørgensen lists the task to improve locational price signals. These aim to
ensure that investments occur where they are most beneficial to the system, while incentivizing an
efficient operation of the system that takes into consideration the grid’s topology.

In this work, we assess three different regulatory scenarios, which correspond to locational price
signals at different spatial resolutions: A Status Quo scenario, representing the current bidding zone
configurations; a Multi Split scenario, which assumes that ambitious bidding zone splits lead to 101
zones; as well as a Locational scenario with locational marginal pricing and locational investment
signals. Both zonal scenarios (Status Quo and Multi Split) are further assessed in combination with
locational investment signals of a high granularity.

We identified significant system security concerns, i.e. substantial curtailment of load, associated with
the Status Quo, as well as the Multi Split scenario – yet at a lower magnitude in the latter case. These
concerns can be traced to an inefficient operation of flexibility – such as storage and demand
response – due to insufficient information on existing bottlenecks being present in the market-
clearing process. Any system cleared with locational marginal pricing, on the other hand, always led
to perfect reliability – even when applied to the investment outcomes of the Status Quo and Multi
Split scenarios.

Our results further suggest substantial cost savings in the context of the Locational and the Multi
Split scenarios. By 2040, the Locational scenario could reduce the total system cost by 26 – 61 Bn.
EUR annually. These savings are in the order of magnitude of those associated with the integration
of European electricity markets. They are a result of lower firm capacity requirements and a higher
utilization of low-carbon energy sources. In addition, consumers in 62-90% of the geographic cells
underpinning the model benefit from more granular pricing in our case study, – depending on the
scenario. Increases beyond 5 EUR/MWh occurred only within up to 1.2% of all cells.

Due to the reliability benefits and the substantial increase in economic efficiency suggested by our
results, we recommend to implement locational investment signals in all relevant investment
mechanisms and introduce locational marginal pricing as an element of the European target model.

Executive summary

Policy context

Grid congestion is becoming an increasingly urgent issue within the European electricity market. In
2023, the Agency for the Cooperation of Energy Regulators (ACER) warned that congestion
management measures of 50 TWh were necessary in 2023 (ACER, 2024). An assessment of the Joint
Research Centre (JRC) further indicated that these redispatch measures could increase to more than
800 TWh and 100 Bn. EUR in 2040 in a scenario with low grid build out and uncoordinated renewable
deployment (Thomaßen et al., 2024). Accordingly, the Mission Letter to Commissioner for Energy and
Affordable Housing Dan Jørgensen includes the task to “improve locational price signals” within the
European electricity system, i.e. price signals with a high spatial granularity (von der Leyen, 2024). In
this context, this work investigates which benefits can be achieved with both, locational operational
price signals, as well as locational investment signals.

Key conclusions

 — Our results suggest that zonal electricity markets struggle to maintain security of supply
   through the market, rendering the constant out-of-the-market procurement of targeted
   reserves necessary. In the Status Quo scenario, large volumes of demand remained unsupplied.
   Substantially lower, yet still highly significant volumes of demand shedding occurred in an ambitious
   bidding zone split scenario (Multi Split). Operating under locational marginal pricing (LMP) always
   resulted in perfect reliability, even when applied to the investment outcome of the Status Quo or Multi
   Split scenarios. Zonal markets require maintaining costly reserve capacity to ensure operational security.

 — Locational price signals were found to lead to substantial economic benefits, with benefits
   being the largest if grid expansion was low. Locational policies can therefore be seen as a
   hedge against insufficient grid build out, yet also achieve substantial savings when grid
   expansion is cost optimal: System cost savings in 2040 were estimated at 26-61 Bn. EUR annually –
   depending on the level of grid build out. The Multi Split scenario achieved substantial benefits as well,
   with system cost savings of 10-37 Bn. EUR/a.

 — Introducing only locational investment signals leads to system cost savings of 12-29 Bn.
   EUR/a in 2040 in a Status Quo setting, highlighting the need to ensure that future deployment
   takes place in the right places. Locational investment signals can be introduced by evaluating
   locational impact as a non-price criterion in renewable auctions and introducing a locational component
   to capacity markets.

 — The largest part of these savings is passed on to consumers, who overwhelmingly benefit
   from locational price signals. The Locational scenario resulted in consumer savings between 24-58
   Bn. EUR/a in 2040, while the Multi Split scenario achieved savings between 9 and 35 Bn. EUR/a.
   Producers also see their profits increase in most cases, and by up to 7%.

 — Given the large identified benefits, we recommend to seriously consider adding LMP to the
   target model, and introduce locational investment signals as soon as possible, given the
   substantial security and cost benefits. This should further be flanked by a reform of European
   forward trading which renders hedging activities independent from specific bidding-zone
   delineations. In addition, an ambitious bidding zone split scenario can already deliver most 2030
   benefits. It needs to be weighed carefully whether the benefits of such a complete reconfiguration of
   many bidding zones prior to the introduction of LMP outweigh the associated costs. On the other hand,
   bidding zone splits that can be implemented without an additional round of the bidding zone review
   could be a low-hanging fruit to reduce congestion inside the European power system.

Main findings

In this work, we assess the evolution of the European power system under three different regulatory
scenarios with regard to the target years 2030 and 2040: (1) A Status Quo scenario, which represents
the current bidding zone configurations; (2) an ambitious bidding-zone-reconfiguration scenario,
leading to 101 zones in the European market coupling algorithm (Multi Split), roughly doubling the
number of bidding zones; as well as (3) a locationally optimized scenario which relies on LMP
operation and locational investment signals (Locational). In addition, both zonal scenarios are
investigated with an implementation of locational investment signals. We further assess three
different levels of grid expansion.




                                                     6
We find that security of supply towards 2040 cannot be maintained through market-based
investments in zonal markets. This is due to flexibility – incentivized by wholesale market prices in
large zones – operating regularly against the system, leading to capacity needs that cannot be
fulfilled at the redispatch stage. This leads to 1.9-4.0 TWh of load curtailment in the Status Quo
scenario, depending on the level of grid expansion. Smaller zones improve the issue – with the Multi
Split scenario resulting in 0.8-1.4 TWh of lost load - while LMP operation always led to perfectly
reliable operation. Zonal operation of the system therefore leads to a substantial and additional need
for capacity to safeguard inefficient trades, which do not achieve a benefit to the system. This implies
as well that capacity remuneration mechanisms would need to take into consideration this need for
additional capacity at the redispatch stage to ensure a secure operation.

The substantial system cost savings are driven by a lower need for firm capacity – as LMP operation
does not require the out-of-the-market procurement of power plant capacity to ensure a feasible
(re)dispatch. The Locational scenario further minimizes the use of combustible fuels, such as coal,
natural gas and hydrogen, by maximizing the integration of low-carbon and low-cost energy carriers.
This does not only reduce costs and emissions but also leads to a lower dependence on fuel imports.

We further find that consumers benefit overwhelmingly from more granular locational pricing. Under
both, the Locational and the Multi Split scenarios, consumers in 62-90% of all geographic cells
underpinning the model benefitted in 2040, compared to the Status Quo scenario, while less than
1.2% of cells saw consumer cost increases beyond 5 EUR/MWh. These higher increases were primarily
limited to some areas in Northern Scandinavia and the Western Balkans.
Our results suggest that an increase in risk for renewable investment in the Locational scenario is
unlikely, since market values remain stable or increase, and can further be addressed by policy action.
In the unlikely case that the introduction of LMP would lead to a risk increase for renewable
investment, substantial cost savings can be expected to remain. The necessary hurdle rate increases
to wipe out all savings were calculated to be between 1.1 and 2.8 % in the Locational scenario
(depending on the level of grid investment). To maximize the benefits from more granular locational
pricing, however, policy measures can be introduced that avoid risk increases to a large degree.
Potential measures include longer remuneration periods and adjustments to the contracts awarded
in renewable auctions. While these measures would increase investment security for renewables, they
would also benefit European consumers as our results suggest that they would lead to lower
electricity bills.

Related JRC work

In 2024, the JRC published an assessment of how redispatch would evolve in the European system,
assuming different grid expansion pathways (Thomaßen et al., 2024). Further activities in this area
include a qualitative assessment of the introduction of nodal pricing in the Internal Electricity Market
in 2020, discussing as well regulatory aspects (Antonopoulos et al., 2020). In the context of the
European market design debate of 2022, the JRC has further proposed an overhaul of renewable and
capacity auctions to include locational information in the auctioning process (Thomaßen et al., 2023).

1 Introduction
The European Union has set out the target to achieve net zero emissions by 2050, a state in which
all remaining carbon emissions would be offset by negative emission technologies or natural sinks.
For the year 2040, the European Commission has proposed a 90% emissions reduction target. This
target basically implies a full decarbonization of the power sector, as abatement costs are lower than
in other hard-to-abate sectors, while simultaneously serving as an input for far-reaching
electrification of the end-use sectors (European Commission, 2024).

The decarbonization of the power sector currently relies largely on the deployment of variable
renewables, such as wind and solar PV. As a side effect of uncoordinated renewable deployment –
allocating capacity asymmetrically across zones – grid congestion has increased substantially within
the European system. Inner-zonal grid congestion can render the outcome of the wholesale market
infeasible, creating the need for TSOs to order the out-of-the-market activation of resources to
maintain the stability of the grid. In 2024, the Agency for the Cooperation of Energy Regulators (ACER)
published a Market Monitoring report, highlighting that 50 TWh of such redispatch measures occurred
in 2023, costing the European consumer 4.3 Bn. EUR (ACER, 2024). An assessment from the JRC
further estimated that redispatch volumes could increase beyond 800 TWh by the year 2040 and
could produce costs of up to 100 Bn. EUR annually under a scenario with low grid expansion
(Thomaßen et al., 2024).

Accordingly, the Mission Letter to the Commissioner for Energy and Affordable Housing Dan
Jørgensen includes the task to “improve locational price signals” within the European electricity
system (von der Leyen, 2024). As a first step into this direction, the Implementing Regulation on
Article 26 of the Net Zero Industry Act (NZIA) features provisions that allow considering the locational
impact of renewable projects within a renewable auction. In this context, (Thomaßen and Fuhrmanek,
2025) have shown that locational investment incentives within a zonal electricity market can
substantially increase the amount of renewable energy integrated into the system, while reducing
redispatch needs and costs to the consumer.

Locational price signals can, however, be improved as well for short-term dispatch decisions.
Especially in the context of flexibility, a higher granularity might be necessary to provide efficient
incentives for load-shifting operations while signalling the right time to storage operators to charge
or feed electricity back to the grid. In the presence of inner-zonal grid congestion, the uniform zonal
price applied to an entire bidding zone regularly does not signal the action most beneficial with regard
to the local conditions in the grid, as a study by the German TSO TenneT suggests (TenneT, 2024).

The Bidding Zone Review (BZR) is the European process that assesses whether the current bidding
zone configurations are still fit fur purpose. According to the Electricity Regulation1, bidding zone
delineations should reflect structural congestion – being taken into consideration at the border – while
redispatch measures should remain a temporary measure. The current iteration of the BZR evaluates
splits of Germany, France, Italy, Sweden and the Netherlands. In this context, the European TSOs
jointly recommended to split the German bidding zone into five, as this configuration achieved the
highest monetary benefit (ENTSO-E, 2025a).

Some TSOs have proposed to take it one step further: Already in 2018, the Polish TSO PSE argued for
the introduction of locational marginal pricing (LMP) at a high spatial granularity (PSE, 2018). They
argued that aligning market outcomes as far as possible with the physical reality was a “precondition
for managing future challenges related to operating the new power system”.

In this study, we assess different approaches to locational price signals and evaluate their overall
efficiency. To this aim, we investigate different combinations of locational investment and locational
operational signals. On the one hand, we assess the impact of different structures of the wholesale
market – one scenario representing the Status Quo, one an ambitious bidding zone split scenario
which roughly doubles the number of zones present in the European electricity market (Multi Split),
and one scenario which depicts the introduction of LMP and locational investment signals (Locational).
On the other hand, we assess a sensitivity that describes the introduction of locational investment
signals to both scenarios that rely on zonal pricing (Status Quo and Multi Split).

2 Theoretical background
In the first part of this section, we dive deeper into the regulatory aspects of introducing different
locational price signals within the European system. In the second part, we discuss and summarize
the literature on the issue, with a focus on relevant aspects for the European discussion.


2.1 Regulatory aspects

2.1.1 Bidding zone reconfiguration
Within the current regulatory framework, the BZR is the instrument through which the spatial
granularity of wholesale market clearing can be increased incrementally. The BZR assesses the
efficiency of the current bidding zone borders and investigates whether a new configuration would
reduce structural congestion inside bidding zones, and would improve market outcomes within the
European electricity market (Thomaßen et al., 2024).
The European TSOs have presented the results of the first iteration – which started in 2020 – in spring
2025 and issued a recommendation to split the German bidding zone into five (the DE5 split).
According to their assessment, this would lead to savings of more than 339 Mio EUR each year. ACER
considers these benefits to be underestimated, as they assume a perfect coordination of remedial
actions, which does not reflect the current practices of congestion management in Europe (ACER,
2025). They found that the actual benefits could be as high as 540 Mio. EUR/a under more realistic
assumptions. As a next step, Member States have to decide whether to follow this recommendation
or whether to maintain the Status Quo.

2.1.2 Locational marginal pricing
Instead of splitting zones iteratively, a far-reaching alignment between physical reality and market
transactions can be achieved through the introduction of LMP. During the 1990s and 2000s, LMP has
been introduced in the US electricity markets and in New Zealand. While there are different
interpretations, we define LMP as a market which is cleared at a sufficiently high spatial granularity
so that market operations are overwhelmingly aligned with the physical reality of the system, i.e. that
very little to no remedial actions remain necessary. This implies that electricity is priced at a high
resolution, reflecting all major transmission constraints within the market clearing algorithm.

In principle, (Antonopoulos et al., 2020) found that LMP could be implemented in the European system
with the existing legislation, as there is no limit to how small a bidding zone could be defined. In
practice, however, successive splitting of zones until an LMP-like system was created appears
impractical to say the least, given that the first iteration of the BZR has taken already five years. It
would likely lead to a protracted period with high levels of uncertainty, since it was unclear how the
future system would exactly look like. Therefore, a successive implementation of LMP in sub-regions
of the European electricity market could present a more feasible option, as proposed by (Neuhoff et
al., 2024). However, this would likely require adapting at least some of the existing legislation, which
could prove a lengthy process (Scott, 2024).

2.1.3 Locational investment signals
Locational investment signals in renewable auctions have previously been implemented in Mexico,
where projects received a mark-up or a penalty depending on their location. These adders were
calculated based on a nodal simulation of the Mexican system, and reflected the average nodal price


                                                  10
in their location. Developing renewables in areas with high production costs therefore provided a
competitive advantage over those projects that were developed in regions with an abundance of low-
cost generation (del Río, 2017).

The Implementing Regulation of Article 26 of NZIA includes provisions that allow to implement
locational investment signals within renewable auctions in the form of non-price criteria. It allows
Member States to evaluate renewable projects based on their expected impact on the grid, benefitting
those projects that would relieve bottlenecks.

In addition, locational investment signals can be implemented within capacity remuneration
mechanisms, such as capacity markets, to ensure that firm capacity is available in the right locations
enabling a feasible (re)dispatch. In PJM (Pennsylvania-New Jersey-Maryland Interconnection) and
CAISO (California Independent System Operator), the capacity market is cleared with several sub-
system-level zones, while Chile performs a clearing of the capacity market at full nodal resolution. In
Europe, Germany, France, Belgium and Ireland have in the past tendered firm capacity to ensure that
import-restricted regions are supplied with enough capacity to maintain a safe operation of the
system (Eicke et al., 2020; Papavasiliou, 2021).


2.2 Summary of the political and the scientific discussion
The current iteration of the BZR – which investigates splitting the German zone into 2-5 smaller zones
(ACER, 2022a) – has triggered a debate in Germany. In this context, twelve leading energy economists
published an open letter to the German government, advocating for wholesale market prices that
reflect the local conditions, and thereby for abandoning the uniform price zone (Hirth et al., 2024).
They argue that a uniform price within a system with structural congestion will set the wrong
operational incentives during many hours, leading to a highly inefficient dispatch. Local prices would
thus not only be important to incentivize efficient siting decisions, but first and foremost to ensure
an efficient operation of the system.

Eleven German industrial associations replied to this appeal, arguing against a split of the German
price zone, highlighting benefits of a large market zone, such as a high liquidity in forward electricity
trading and stable expectations of what price level new investments can expect years ahead (BEE et
al., 2024). Currently, forward trading activities in the European Union largely focus on the largest
bidding zones. To detach the issue of bidding zone reconfigurations from forward markets, ACER has
proposed to establish a virtual trading hub for the Core region, similar to the one established in the
Nordics (ACER, 2023a).

In their investigation of the proposed German bidding zone splits, (Tiedemann et al., 2024) found that
some generators – primarily wind in renewable-surplus regions – would see a substantial decrease
in their market value. This has been confirmed in a UK context by (AFRY, 2023) who see risks for
investments on the generation-surplus side of a bottleneck, potentially resulting in system cost
increases if these risks were to increase hurdle rates for renewable investments. They identified a
lower market value during the merchant tail – the period after the remuneration contract has ended
– as a substantial cause. Volume risks were further identified by (Blyth et al., 2024) as a potential
cost driver for clearing prices in renewable auctions: Smaller zones can increase the risk that operators
will be unable to sell their electricity, due to a surplus of renewable energy occurring locally. If the
remuneration contract is coupled to the actual production, this implies that also no subsidy will be
paid during these periods. (Tiedemann et al., 2024) accordingly concluded that any bidding zone
reconfiguration should therefore be accompanied by an appropriate compensation mechanism, for
example through the introduction of contracts that are based on virtual reference plants, rather than



                                                   11
actual production volumes (Newbery, 2023; Schlecht et al., 2024). The performance of these virtual
reference plants would be calculated based on the (theoretical) energy production of a similar plant
under the realized weather conditions.

At the same time, they found that consumers were only marginally affected by any of the investigated
bidding zone reconfigurations. This aligns with the findings of (Knörr et al., 2024), who investigated
the proposed German splits in a 2025 setting, finding no substantial impact on average wholesale
prices. They further identified price differences between different locations as beneficial for
supporting the system’s stability. This effect was largest in a setting with locational marginal pricing
(LMP): While the system effects of storage operation in large bidding zones appear to be arbitrary at
best (TenneT, 2024), the highly-granular price signals in LMP operation employ storage and other
flexibility to manage congestions and relieve the grid, becoming a pillar of the system’s security.
Accordingly, (Knörr et al., 2024) identified operational cost savings of 7-14% through LMP.

In a previous assessment, (Neuhoff et al., 2013) estimated 1.1-3.6% operational cost savings through
LMP in a 2013 setting. This could indicate that savings increase with higher levels of decarbonization,
potentially due to dispatchable generation becoming more costly, a higher penetration of flexibility,
such as storage and demand response and a higher volatility in prices through low-cost variable
renewable generation. (Lété et al., 2022) further modelled the longer-term evolution of the CWE
(Central Western Europe) system until 2035. They concluded that a locationally optimized system
would decrease the cost of the entire system by 3%, indicating that the long-run effects can be even
larger, if not only operational but also investment decisions take into consideration the grid’s topology.
This is confirmed by (Thomaßen and Fuhrmanek, 2025) which found that locationally optimized
renewable expansion could lead to large system cost savings and consumer benefits, even if European
markets continue operating within their current bidding zone configuration. (Brown et al., 2020),
however, question whether LMP alone could incentivize locationally optimal investment patterns,
which could suggest that additional locational investment mechanisms are required to achieve
efficient deployment patterns (Eicke et al., 2020).

These findings generally align with non-EU studies that identified substantial cost savings from an
increase in spatial granularity in market-clearing operations. In the UK, the topic has been debated
extensively in the context of a review of the electricity market design: (FTI Consulting and Energy
Systems Catapult, 2023) found that an ambitious split scenario of the UK zone could achieve 6-15
Bn. GBP (~7-18 Bn. EUR) of welfare benefits until 2040, with consumers benefitting in each region of
the UK, while reducing emissions by 65-100 Mt of CO2. An introduction of LMP was estimated to
deliver between 28 and 51 Bn. GBP (~33-61 Bn. EUR) within the same time horizon. An update of the
study – which considered only operational impacts, without regulatory changes affecting the locations
where investments occurred – found that a more ambitious bidding zone split scenario could deliver
54 Bn. GBP (~64 Bn. EUR) of consumer benefits until 2050 (FTI Consulting, 2025). These results were
disputed by (AFRY, 2025, 2023), which estimated the benefits of comparable bidding zone splits only
at 3.3 Bn. GBP (~3.9 Bn. EUR) until 2040, and the benefits of an introduction of LMP at 4.5 Bn. GBP
(~5.3 Bn. EUR) until 2050. A key difference in the two approaches appears to be the assumption of
unconstrained grid expansion post 2035 in (AFRY, 2025, 2023), indicating that constrained grid
expansion may lead to a higher comparative advantage of increased locational price signals
compared to uniform pricing . This is confirmed as well by both consultancies: (FTI Consulting, 2023),
noted that the results by (AFRY, 2023) imply that an ambitious bidding zone split scenario could
deliver consumer benefits of 14 Bn. GBP (~17 Bn. EUR) and an increase of 9 Bn. GBP (~11 Bn. EUR)
in overall welfare in a scenario where transmission build out was delayed by five years.

Outside of Europe, several assessments have concluded with similar findings. In the US, where all
electricity markets have implemented LMP, ex-post analyses have estimated operational cost savings
of 2.1% in California and 3.9% in Texas. (NERA, 2020) further assessed the introduction of LMP in
the Australian electricity market, considering both, the impact on operations and on investment. They
estimated a 14% reduction in the system’s cost until 2040, with a similar additional benefit to
consumers driven by a reduction in producer surplus. This indicates that the long-run benefits of
spatially optimized investment and operational decisions combined could accumulate to significant
consumer and efficiency benefits.

In addition to welfare impacts, the market clearing resolution appears to impact the system’s
reliability with increasing deployment of distributed renewables. Experts from the German TSO
Amprion have published an article stating that the security of the system might be threatened if
market signals do not reflect the physical reality in the grid (Sapp et al., 2024). Aligning with this
assessment, the Polish TSO PSE has in the past concluded that a transition to LMP operation would
be essential to remain able to manage a system which consists of large volumes of distributed
resources (PSE, 2018). Instead of aligning market operations better with the physical reality, (Sapp et
al., 2024), however, propose to limit the operating ranges of power plants, effectively introducing
limits to free electricity trade. Such an approach, if implemented, could undermine the liberalization
of European electricity markets as it would be up to the TSO to decide which power plants would be
allowed – or even mandated – to operate, and which not.

2.2.1 Key take aways
Several assessments indicate that locational price signals can achieve significant welfare benefits, if
they lead to efficient operational and/or investment decisions. This effect appears to increase with
decarbonization. In a system that consists of large numbers of distributed generators, storage and
flexible consumers – potentially down to the household level – a higher granularity appears to be
beneficial for coordinating short-term operations. In fact, an effective integration could make
flexibility a key pillar of the system’s reliability.

Risks were identified with regard to the predictability of future wholesale prices, which could
potentially undermine hedging activities and increase investment risks. This indicates that any reform
should be accompanied by a reform of European forward markets that ensure continuity as well in
cases when bidding zones are reconfigured, for example through the introduction of virtual trading
hubs (ACER, 2023a). To maximize the benefits from more granular locational pricing, it appears
further to be key to ensure a stable investment environment. This concerns especially renewable
investments, as renewable production is foreseen to scale up massively over the next decades
(European Commission, 2024). An increase in their capital costs could thus disproportionally affect
the total cost of the system.

3 Methodology
In this section, we describe the methodology that was applied in the case study. We rely on a 1024
node model generated with the standard PyPSA-Eur workflow – which uses a k-means clustering
algorithm based on the physical location of the substations – and using climate data for the year
2013 as the base model. Country level load curves are furthermore distributed based on GDP and
population inside each cell (Hörsch et al., 2018). The PyPSA model uses energy balances for various
components, such as buses, generators, storage units, lines and links, and determines the least-cost
dispatch through linear optimization. For the full set of equations, we refer the reader to (PyPSA,
2025), which features an extensive documentation. The additional features, developed for this case
study, are further described in this section.

Based on this model, we build representations of the European system in 2030 and 2040, in line with
the Impact Assessment for the proposed 2040 target with regard to key indicators such as total
demand per type (inflexible, heat pumps and electric vehicles), as well as installed renewables and
electrolysis capacity (European Commission, 2024). We further assume that carbon neutrality is
achieved in the power system by 2040.
For the zonal scenarios, representations of zonal market configurations are generated, which are
cleared using a representation of flow-based market coupling (FBMC). In a second step, redispatch
optimization is performed to achieve a physically feasible dispatch. The corresponding computational
routines are described in this section. They further represent an evolutionary development of the
workflow applied in (Thomaßen et al., 2024).


3.1 Scenarios
In this case study, we compare different regulatory scenarios with respect to the spatial resolution at
which the wholesale market is cleared. The baseline, against which all other scenarios are compared,
is the Status Quo scenario, which reflects the current bidding zone configurations of the European
power system. Cross-zonal flows are allocated with the help of FBMC, while bottlenecks are relieved
through redispatch2.
The second scenario represents an ambitious bidding-zone-split scenario, which might be attained
through several iterations of the BZR – or one ambitious application. This Multi Split scenario thus
assumes a strong increase in the number of bidding zones, leading to 101 zones3 and therefore goes
beyond the currently proposed bidding zone reconfigurations. These zones have been generated
through the PyPSA network clustering functionality using a k-means clustering algorithm. The
corresponding configuration can be seen in Figure 2. Every new zone created within the Multi Split
scenario has an equal or larger electricity demand than the smallest zone in the current zonal
configuration of the European electricity market. The overall market structure remains the same as
in the Status Quo scenario, including FBMC to allocate cross zonal capacities, and redispatch for
congestion management.

3.1.1 Investment incentives
We further assume that all investment instruments reflect at least the geospatial resolution of market
clearing. This assumption was chosen since it makes little sense to introduce spatial price signals at
a high resolution within short-term markets, while relying on investment mechanisms that would then




                                                    15
again blunt the resulting investment signals4. It appears to be common sense that such a scenario
would yield worse results than a scenario in which investment signals reflect at least the spatial
resolution of the market. Therefore, this combination was not investigated. At the same time, we
always need to consider that the results presented in this case study do not only reflect a spatial
market clearing resolution, but as well a corresponding type of investment incentives.

In addition, we assess a sensitivity which investigates the effect of having locational incentives for
investment within zonal market configurations. This sensitivity therefore assumes that investment
into renewables, dispatchable generation and storage occurs in the same spot as it would in the
Locational scenario, while the market is operated at the respective spatial resolution of the scenario
(Status Quo or Multi Split). It implies a locational design of renewable auctions, capacity markets and
potential flexibility investment mechanisms.

It has to be highlighted that the locational incentives investigated in this case study assume improving
the business case for deployment in areas which are beneficial to the grid. They do not imply
restrictions on building capacity in any region, but assume instead that grid location is considered in
the clearing process of renewable auctions, capacity markets, or other financing mechanisms. This is
done by giving a higher value – explicitly or through a locational non-price criterion – to projects that
relieve congestion.


3.2 Investment and cash flow modelling
To reach the 90% emission reduction target by 2040, large volumes of investment into power system
infrastructure are necessary. To depict this, we model investment into renewables, dispatchable
resources and storage, as well as grids. The basis for these investment decisions is a time-reduced
model, similar to (FTI Consulting and Energy Systems Catapult, 2023; NERA, 2020). In each scenario,
generator investment is calculated using models that represent the respective market configuration.
In the Locational scenario, as well as when locational investment signals are being applied, this
corresponds to the entire grid model. In the Multi Split and Status Quo scenarios without locational
investment signals, generators and storage units were connected to aggregated nodes, which
represent the respective zonal configuration.

3.2.1 Renewable generation
On a country level, the capacities for each renewable technology are the same in each scenario for
the same target year. The scenarios differ, however, where inside each country the capacity is
installed.

renewable capacity, which is achieved by adding a constraint to the investment model, enforcing that
a certain level of capacity for each technology has to be installed in each zone, while minimizing the
total cost of the system. These auction targets are aligned with the Impact Assessment regarding the
proposed 2040 climate target.

3.2.2 Contracts for difference
We further assume that all renewable and nuclear assets are remunerated through a two-way
contract for difference (CfD) which is referencing the local energy price. If the market price is lower
than the strike price, the asset in question therefore receives a payment which makes up the
difference. If the market price exceeds the strike price, however, the operator has to pay back the
difference between the two. The strike price is further determined in a way that all assets are fully
refinanced, while the cost of these contracts is taken into consideration separately during the cost-
benefit analysis.

In the Status Quo and Multi Split scenarios, these reference prices, against which the contracts are
settled, correspond to the electricity price of the bidding zone that the asset is installed in. In the
Locational scenario, it refers to the price of the node that the asset is connected to. CfD strike prices
and payments are calculated ex post, after the market dispatch runs, and based on the resulting
prices and energy volumes produced. We further assume that energy production is only remunerated
for as long as the market price is positive, and that CfD contracts are revenue sufficient, i.e. that they
are fully covering the total cost of the asset, without providing substantial additional rents – as to be
expected if the renewable auctions are competitive. The strike price of the CfD was therefore

3.2.3.1 Grid reserves

In the context of our case study, we observed that both zonal scenarios resulted in significant amounts
of involuntary load shedding at the redispatch stage, while sufficient capacities were available in all
scenarios to ensure the market always cleared. This implies that the electricity market would not be
capable of incentivizing additional investment, as these capacities would remain idle during the
market sessions and therefore would not generate a margin. The redispatch stage, on the other hand
would not provide additional revenues, as we assume that congestion management is remunerated
on a cost basis, as currently the practice, for example, in Germany (Hirth et al., 2019). The modelling
procedure for the cost-based redispatch mechanism is further described in section 3.4.

In reality, regulators would surely intervene in time to ensure that sufficient capacities were available
to safeguard the operation of the system. In fact, this situation has already occurred, with Germany
auctioning off the construction of four new power plants to provide redispatch services only, without
the permission to sell energy to the market (Eicke et al., 2020).

The necessary grid reserves to safeguard the operation of the system are computed using the
redispatch model as the basis (compare section 3.4). This network comprises of the nodal net
positions as determined by the market simulation, as well as all existing redispatch options. We
further add investment options for hydrogen-ready CCGT and OCGT plants to each node. We then
optimize the investment decisions to determine the capacity level needed to ensure a safe supply.

3.2.4 Hydrogen
In a deviation from the assumptions applied in (Thomaßen et al., 2024), we further assume that
hydrogen generation is correlated with renewable surplus. This change has been introduced after a
screening of the Rystad Hydrogen Solution database, which indicates that planned hydrogen projects
often correlate with high-renewable-production nodes. Correlating electrolysis capacity with
renewable surplus nodes will minimize the impact of electrolysis on redispatch needs, as it maximizes
the local utilization of renewables (compare, for example, (50Hertz et al., 2023; Vom Scheidt et al.,
2022)). It can be therefore assumed that this assumption will lower the cost estimates for zonal
markets: A lower correlation with renewable surplus than assumed in this study would lead to a higher
discrepancy between wholesale market clearing and actual dispatch, causing an additional need for
redispatch.

3.2.5 Revenue gap
Lastly, we evaluate the revenue gap for dispatchable generation and storage. The underlying
assumption is that investors will not invest into new power plants unless they are convinced that
these are sufficiently profitable. Since we assume adequate levels of capacity to meet the demand
in the market, no scarcity price signals occur. Several Member States are further planning to introduce
a capacity market – or have already done so – among them France, Poland, Belgium, Germany and
Spain. This funding stream, which we do not model, comes on top of the revenue from the market



                                                   20
and would likely be sufficient to match any missing money, since it is determined through an
auctioning process. In addition, the Electricity Market Design reform foresees the possibility to
establish remuneration schemes for flexibility, which could cover any missing money for storage
investments.

Assessing a system without refinanced assets would underestimate the cost of the system, as it
cannot be assumed that these assets would remain in the market. Adequate revenues therefore need
to be assumed to correctly estimate the cost to consumers. By removing unused capacity, we further
ensure that no overcapacity is being considered and all units are needed for market and/or redispatch
operations. We thus consider it a reasonable assumption that the revenue gap would be borne by
consumers either (1) because investment would be marginally less5, so that scarcity prices would
occur on a scale that is sufficient to render all remaining assets profitable. Or (2) because additional
funding mechanisms, such as capacity markets or flexibility schemes, would provide the missing
funds.

3.2.6 Grids

3.2.6.1 Grid expansion

For each regulatory scenario, the grid expansion needs are determined individually. We therefore do
not assume that grid expansion plans are set in stone, but that they can be adapted in case the needs
of the system change. The advantage of the approach is twofold: On the one hand, grid planning
inside European Member States remains unfortunately rather opaque. National network development
plans (NDPs) are commonly published in the national language only, and on several occasions lack
concrete information on the grid projects – such as the concrete number, capacity and location of
HVDC lines6. On the other hand, we do not evaluate how well a given market design fits with the
current plans, but we take into consideration that these plans could be adjusted, in case the resulting
resource mix requires less or different expansion measures. We do, however, rely on NDPs for the
consideration of new HVDC lines. To this aim, we consider candidate projects, taken from the ten-year
network development plan (TYNDP), as well as national NDPs (50Hertz et al., 2023; ENTSO-E, 2025b;
PSE, 2024).

In addition, we assume the roll out of dynamic line rating across the European power system, applying
the standard PyPSA-Eur functionality (Glaum and Hofmann, 2023). We further enforced a lower limit
that corresponds to the static line rating, as well as an upper limit that corresponds to a 50% increase
in capacity. Increasing this level further has little effect on additional grid capacity (Glaum and
Hofmann, 2023), while it ensures we stay within the limit deemed safe for operations (ENTSO-E,
2024). This threshold was confirmed to us as a reasonable assumption in the context of bilateral
discussions with experts from the field.

3.3.1 Optimized GSK calculation
Today, GSKs are usually calculated by considering that large dispatchable or hydro power plants will
be the major contributors to a change in net position (Elia, 2023; ENTSO-E, 2023b). This is a valid
approach for as long as renewable curtailment is low and battery penetration is still rather small. The
more renewable surplus periods occur, the more likely a change in net position will deviate from this
approximation, as renewables will be the first sources to contribute to a change in net positions.
We can, for example, consider a case where the potential generation from variable renewables
exceeded the domestic demand by 30%. In this case, all variable renewables would bid into the
market at near zero marginal cost, making it already hard to predict which plants would be dispatched
in the base case. A change in net position – for example increasing exports due to the large availability
of low-cost energy – would, in turn, then be first and foremost provided by the unused variable
renewable generation, which would otherwise end up being curtailed. Conventional GSKs would,
however, implicitly assume that large scale conventional generators would provide the additional
output. If the location of these generators does not align with those of the variable renewable
generators providing the exported power, the calculated flows on the CNECs will inevitably be wrong.
This issue is further exacerbated with increasing levels of decentralized storage applications, such as
household batteries or medium scale storage installed together with wind parks or solar farms. In
such a system, a change in net positions cannot accurately be represented only by the distribution of
large power plants.

For systems with high renewable shares, the conventional GSK approach thus appears less suitable.
We therefore optimistically assume an evolution of FBMC in which TSOs can predict with a high
accuracy which nodes will contribute to a change in net position during a given hour. To this aim, we
run two dispatch models, one with a simplified NTC approach, as well as one without any transfer
capacity between zones. This allows us to estimate better which nodes contribute to a net position
change during a given hour:

The GSK therefore considers the contribution of all power system elements, including conventional

In itself, the approach presented here is quite optimistic and can hardly be taken as a given. It
assumes that TSOs will be able to forecast with a high accuracy how thousands of solar farms or
wind parks will generate, as well as how households will operate their batteries and charge their
electric vehicles. In this case study, however, we want to assess the zonal market concept considering
as well efficiency benefits that could be achieved in the future. It remains therefore necessary to
remember that the FBMC described in this study is an optimized version, while the current
implementation would likely perform worse.

3.4.1 Improved cross-zonal congestion management
We further assume that efficient congestion management across market zones is possible in all zonal
scenarios (Status Quo and Multi Split). In each country, these measures are allowed to provide up to
30% of all remedial actions, which can already be considered an optimistic evolution of
countertrading. According to (ACER, 2023b), countertrading accounted only for 15% of all redispatch
costs.

In addition, we assume that the effectiveness of cross-zonal remedial actions increases as well.
Classical countertrading limits TSOs to buying energy from another bidding zone, without knowing
where exactly this additional energy will come from. We assume that TSOs can activate resources in
different bidding zones in a targeted way to relieve bottlenecks in their own, which implies a
substantially higher level of cross-border cooperation than conventional countertrading.

3.6 Limitations to the approach
The results presented here assume a certain evolution of the European system with regard to the
target years 2030 and 2040. While we believe that we depict a reasonable and realistic evolution of
the system, unforeseen trends could materialize which could affect the outcome of this exercise
qualitatively and quantitatively. This could relate to the locations that investors choose to deploy
renewables, or to the technologies they invest in: It is hardly possible to assess every cost factor that
might influence siting decisions across Europe. Therefore, real deployment patterns may differ from
the ones assumed in this study. Further, assuming for example a European power system dominated
by carbon-neutral dispatchable generation, such as Small Modular Reactors (SMRs) or biomass would
certainly lead to very different results.

We furthermore assume a certain evolution of the European market design with regard to how the
market is cleared at the day-ahead stage, and how congestion is relieved throughout the European
system. Considering the full range of potential regulatory architectures that could materialize goes
beyond the scope of this exercise. Limiting, for example, the operating ranges of generators and
storage units, as discussed by (Sapp et al., 2024), could reduce congestion, yet would simultaneously
imply new limits to the free trade of electricity. A less-efficient FBMC or a less-efficient cross-zonal
redispatch, on the other hand, could lead to more costly congestion relief than shown in this report.

4 Results
This section presents the results of the case study. First, we analyse the impact of market design on
grid expansion needs, before assessing operations in each scenario – evaluating indicators on supply
security, fuel mix and storage operation. We then discuss capacity needs of the system, before
evaluating the impact on the system cost, consumers and producers.


4.1 Grid expansion needs
The different scenarios result in different grid expansion needs – which we define as the system
optimal level of grid expansion. The overall needs calculated for the Status Quo scenario compare
well with the costs identified by national reports: Our model results in onshore grid expansion costs
of 86 Bn. EUR10 in Germany, compared to 94.6 Bn. EUR according to the NDP (50Hertz et al., 2023).
In France, onshore grid expansion was calculated to be 59.3 Bn. EUR, while the French NDP lists 70.0
Bn. EUR in onshore measures (RTE, 2025). The somewhat higher costs in these NDPs can likely be
attributed to several factors: On the one hand, we do not model grid expansion until all bottlenecks
are relieved, but only up to the cost optimum. This cost-optimal level may thus still include some
structural congestion, if alleviating these bottlenecks through additional expansion is not cost
efficient. On the other hand, we optimize grid expansion measures in all countries at once, which
implies a level of coordination that exceeds the current status quo for network planning, where each
country is planning their own network. It is therefore possible that our model harvests synergies that
are currently not attained in national network planning. Further differences might be attributed to
differences in scenario assumptions, among other factors, for example with regard to the
geographical distribution of renewables, the location of new demands, as well as their flexibility.

Increasing the geographical resolution at which the European market is cleared can substantially
reduce the necessary volume of grid investment. The Locational scenario leads to an overall reduction
of grid expansion needs by 56 Bn. EUR by 2040. The largest share of these cost savings can already
be achieved through the Multi Split scenario, which leads to overall cost reductions of 53 Bn. EUR.

4.2 Operations
In this section, we first address the security of supply achieved in the different regulatory scenarios,
before discussing differences in fuel mix and in the operation of flexibility.

Both zonal scenarios resulted in substantial volumes of lost load. Figure 6 shows the amount of
involuntary load shedding per scenario and target year. For the Locational scenario, the dispatch
resulted always in a perfectly reliable supply and therefore no load shedding volumes are reported.
All load shedding events reported for the zonal runs took place during the redispatch stage, since
sufficient capacities were available to clear the market at all times (compare section 3.2). As we can
see, the Status Quo scenario results in substantially higher load shedding than the Multi Split scenario,
indicating that a 101-zone system already improves the security of supply substantially. While the
2030 runs result in 7-24 GWh of load shedding in the Status Quo scenario, the Multi Split scenario
resulted only in 3.5-8 GWh with lost load. In 2040, load shedding is, however, occurring on another
scale: In the Status Quo scenario, 1.9 TWh of load shedding occur – representing 0.03% of total
demand – even under system optimal grid expansion. If inner-country grid expansion is constrained
to 20%, this amount increases to 4.0 TWh (0.07% of total demand). At the same time, the Multi Split
scenario resulted in 0.8-1.4 TWh of lost load, representing between 0.01% and 0.03% of total
demand.

These results suggest that larger zones lead to a poorer supply situation. At the same time, they
indicate that even an ambitious bidding zone split scenario leads to substantial amounts of load
shedding, if no additional out-of-the-market actions are implemented to combat these scarcity
situations in a targeted way.

We can understand the nature of these load shedding events better by considering their geographical
distribution, as shown exemplarily in Figure 7 for the Status Quo scenario in 2040 with optimal grid
expansion. These take place overwhelmingly on the periphery of the European system, which can be
explained by the fact that there is less flexibility in these locations to deal with a shortage in supply
at the redispatch stage: A shortage on a border between two bidding zones might still be compensated
through cross-border cooperation. This option, however, does not exist at the borders of the system.
If insufficient redispatch options are available locally, it will therefore result in load shedding. It has
to be highlighted that our simulations involve an optimized cross-border cooperation during the
redispatch stage, which is currently not a given (compare section 3.4.1). With less efficient cross-
border remedial actions, additional load shedding events could take place as well further in the centre
of the European system.

The fact that the LMP operation of the same systems led to no load shedding indicates that sufficient
flexibility is in general available to safeguard the operation of the system, even if no locational
investment signals apply, i.e. even if investment occurs in suboptimal locations. In zonal markets with

4.2.1 Capacity needs
The different operational patterns result in strongly differing capacity needs, which can be seen in
Figure 10. The Locational scenario requires 59-64 GW less firm capacity for a safe operation of the
system, which can be explained by the improved integration of decentralized flexibility. Yet also the
Multi Split scenario results in 11-33 GW lower firm capacity needs. We can further observe that the
capacity need decreases continuously with grid build out.

There are thus two factors at play that minimize the firm capacity need of the system: On the one
hand, a higher spatial resolution in market clearing enables more targeted employment of flexibility,
thereby decreasing the need for firm capacity to safeguard the system. Likewise, higher grid
expansion enables a better utilization of those capacities that are available, leading to an overall
lower requirement.

4.4 Producer profits
Across all scenarios, we can observe that a higher spatial granularity in market clearing increases the
profits of generators. This can be explained by the fact that the redispatch action is partially (Multi
Split) or completely (Locational) internalized. Since we assume cost-based redispatch, redispatch
activities do not generate any profits, while an activation in the market can generate a margin for all
cheaper resources. In addition, it might even allow the resource in question to generate a margin, in
case an even costlier redispatch measure was internalized as well during the same time period.

Assuming that generators would generate profits through redispatch action could, of course, alter this
picture. This could, for example, be the case due to opaque costs on the site of generation companies,
which could lead to regulators accepting cost estimates that are higher than the real costs. Since
consumers would have to bear these additional costs, this case would represent a redistribution from
consumers to producers. In this way, generator profits would benefit less from more granular
locational pricing, as profits would already be higher in the Status Quo scenario, while consumer bills
would be reduced even more substantially.

Further, the profits of storage operators are increased in every configuration of the Locational
scenario. This indicates that they can generate additional revenues by contributing to congestion
management. In 2040, this applies to the Multi Split scenario as well, while storage operators see
losses or no change in 2030.

The largest increase in profits for both technology classes occurs in the case that grid expansion is
constrained. By 2040, both the Multi Split and the Locational scenario increase profits by roughly 2.5
Bn. EUR, which represents roughly a 7% increase compared to the Status Quo scenario.

5 Discussion

5.1 Supply security
LMP operation appears to be essential to maintaining security of supply at lowest cost in low-carbon
electricity systems with a high share of flexibility. This is due to the fact that the coordinated operation
of flexible assets requires price signals at a high spatial granularity to achieve an efficient dispatch.
With zonal pricing, we identified substantial deviations from optimal operational patterns of flexibility,
on some occasions operating against the system’s needs. A higher spatial granularity in wholesale
pricing is therefore essential to signal to storage operators and flexible consumers what actions fit
best with the local needs.

As a result of inefficient integration, we identified severe risks to the security of supply, resulting in
up to 4 TWh of load shedding in 2040 (0.07% of total demand). All of these load shedding events
occurred at the redispatch stage. Since LMP operation of the same system resulted in perfect
reliability, these issues can be attributed to the operation of the system, and are not primarily related
to investment in the wrong places. It is therefore not a matter of insufficient availability of resources,
but rather an issue how the operation of these resources is coordinated. The Multi Split scenario, on
the other hand, resulted in substantially lower load shedding, indicating that ambitious bidding zone
splits can be an important step towards improving the security of supply.

This indicates that zonal markets with large bidding zones struggle with maintaining security of supply
through the market. Substantial out-of-the-market action becomes necessary to ensure that enough
power plant capacities are available to provide the necessary redispatch. Our results suggest that the
Locational scenario, which assumes locational operation and investment signals, could result in up to
64 GW less firm capacity needed to safeguard operations. Ambitious bidding zone splits, as assumed
in the Multi Split scenario, could already reduce firm capacity requirements by up to 33 GW. This
additional capacity need, triggered by the operation of flexibility, must be procured through out-of-
the-market mechanisms, such as capacity markets or designated grid reserves. ENTSO-E has already
acknowledged the need for these grid reserves, as indicated by the results of the BZR (ENTSO-E,
2025a).

Generally, it has been confirmed as well by some TSOs – or associated experts – that the decoupling
between market trade and physical reality can lead to supply security issues (PSE, 2018; Sapp et al.,
2024). Coincidentally, the two TSO(-associated) publications outline the two directions of policy action
through which the issue could be mitigated: While (PSE, 2018) argue for the introduction of LMP – a
market-based solution – (Sapp et al., 2024) advocate for limiting the operating ranges of power plants
through the introduction of so-called feasibility ranges. This would mean that TSOs define how power
plant operators are allowed operate their plants, undermining the liberalization of the electricity sector
to a large degree. With the issue of grid congestion becoming increasingly dominant in the future, the
portion handled by the market would likely shrink successively. There is an additional risk, since TSOs
are not market operators and therefore have a different expertise than NEMOs. It is therefore not a
given that the prescribed feasibility ranges would automatically optimize the cost of the system.
There could further be an incentive for TSOs to prioritize short-term operations over, for example, the
optimal intertemporal dispatch of flexible assets, such as storages or demand response.




                                                    48
5.2 System cost and welfare effects
Since less power plants are needed to “correct” inefficient operations from flexible assets, the
Locational and the Multi Split scenario both reduce the need to burn costly fuels. On the contrary,
storage and demand response contribute effectively to integrating higher shares of low-carbon
electricity from renewables or nuclear power plants. This reduces the need for burning fossil fuels in
2030 and hydrogen in 2040. It is fair to assume that a large share of these fuels would need to be
imported, therefore we can anticipate that price signals of a higher spatial granularity would likely
reduce the dependency on energy imports by increasing the utilization of domestic low-carbon energy
sources.

Lower capacity requirements and lower needs for combustible fuels result in substantially lower costs
in the Locational and the Multi Split scenario than in the Status Quo scenario. In 2040, the Locational
scenario leads to cost reductions of up to 61 Bn. EUR annually if inner-country grid expansion is
capped at 20%. But even under optimal grid expansion, savings would still amount to 26 Bn. EUR
each year. For comparison, the annual benefits of European electricity market integration were
estimated at 34 Bn. EUR (ACER, 2022b), thus of a similar magnitude. A substantial amount of the
estimated savings can already be achieved through an ambitious bidding zone split scenario: The
Multi Split scenario resulted in cost savings between 10 and 37 Bn. EUR annually in 2040.

Similarly, locational investment signals can contribute to unlocking a part of these cost savings. They
were found to provide the largest benefit in the Status Quo setting, with today’s bidding zone
configuration. In these cases, they were able to reduce annual system costs by 12-29 Bn. EUR.
Analogous to locational operational price signals, their effect is largest when introduced in a setting
with constrained grid expansion. Both, LMP and locational investment signals can therefore be
considered also as a hedge against less-than-optimal grid expansion.
Our results further suggest that both, consumers and producers receive a net benefit from the
identified cost savings. Analysing the distribution of consumer cost reductions, we can further
conclude that the overwhelming part of European consumers – in 62-90% of the geographical cells
that underpin the model – would benefit from lower electricity costs by 2040 in both, the Locational
and the Multi Split scenario. A cost increase beyond 5 EUR/MWh was identified only in up to 1.2% of
all cells. These larger cost increases occurred primarily in the Western Balkans, as well as in Northern
Scandinavia.
Further, overall profits increase in almost all cases, with the only exception being the Multi Split
scenario under optimal grid expansion. In general, constrained grid expansion appears to be increasing
profits for generators and storage, as less grid capacity is available to reduce price arbitrage between
different locations.


5.3 Impact on investment conditions for renewables
Our results do not suggest that a reform in line with the Locational scenario would increase risks for
renewable investments during the merchant tail phase. To the contrary, we find that an introduction
of LMP, in combination with spatially optimized deployment actually increases the market value of
offshore wind and solar PV in general, as well as onshore wind with a 2030 perspective. Towards
2040, onshore wind saw a minimal decrease, below 1%. If anything, one could argue that the
increased market value during the merchant tail phase could therefore rectify a lower cost of capital.
(Please note that we did not consider any changes in the hurdle rate due to the increased profitability
in the Locational scenario.) Break even hurdle rates for the Locational scenario are furthermore large,
reducing the likelihood that a risk increase for renewable investments would erase all efficiency gains.


                                                  49
Nonetheless, policy action should aim to avoid or at least minimize risk increases to maximize the
benefits from more granular locational pricing.

The situation is somewhat different in the Multi Split scenario. Here we find more decreases in market
value for wind technologies, while solar PV remains a beneficiary of reforms under most
configurations. The Multi Split scenario could therefore indeed increase the risk of investing into wind
generation, which represents a clear disadvantage compared to the other scenarios. An ambitious
bidding zone split scenario, if to be implemented, should therefore be mitigated by appropriate policy
action to increase revenue stability for these investments. At the same time the break-even hurdle
rate increase under optimal grid expansion was estimated at only 0.4%, indicating a non-neglectable
risk that efficiency gains could be wiped out by a risk increase for renewables, if system-optimal
volumes of grid expansion are achieved. This increases the need for appropriate policy action if an
ambitious bidding zone split scenario should be envisaged (compare section 6.4).

6 Policy options
The results presented in the previous sections suggest strongly that locational operational price
signals of a higher spatial granularity are necessary to achieve a market design that ensures a safe
operation of the European power system without the need for constant redispatching, while keeping
the cost of the system low. Our results further suggest that locational investment signals are a no-
regret option, which can reduce the cost of the system, especially when clearing the wholesale market
at a coarse spatial granularity. In the following, we discuss the three main policy options going
forward, which are: (1) the widespread introduction of locational investment signals, (2) a gradual
increase in market clearing resolution by splitting bidding zones, as well as (3) a transition to LMP.
Please note that (1) can (and potentially should) be combined with the other two options. We further
discuss how to de-risk renewable investment – which is foreseen to scale up massively in the coming
decades – in the context of potential reforms towards more granular locational pricing to maximize
their benefits.


6.1 Locational investment signals
Our results suggest that substantial benefits can be achieved by implementing locational investment
signals that lead to spatially-optimized investment decisions. Given the high benefits, as well as the
comparably low cost of implementation, we consider locational investment signals to be a no-regret
option, which can be implemented regardless of whether the granularity of operational price signals
should be increased. Implementing policies that steer capacity to those locations where it is most
beneficial to the system can be considered the highest priority in the current context, as the coming
decades decide how the system will be set up. If the massive roll-out of renewables and storage
focusses on the wrong locations, it would result in a costly system which would be tedious to correct.

A locational non-price criterion (compare section 2.1.3) enables Member States to pick those projects
in an auction process that provide the largest benefit to the system, considering the impact on grid
congestion. Achieving a meaningful impact within renewable auctions would further rely on giving
said mechanism an adequate weight, in comparison to other non-price criteria but also with regard
to the bid price. In addition, it should be considered to mandate that locational impact has to be
considered in any renewable auction which covers a region where structural grid congestion is present.

Even if the granularity of market clearing will be increased, through bidding zone splits or a full-scale
introduction of LMP, locational auctions should be implemented, since previous research suggests
that the effectiveness of short-term operational signals on steering capacity to the most beneficial
locations is limited (Brown et al., 2020). Incentivizing CfD-hedged resources to move towards the
right locations without a locational auction mechanism would further mean exposing them to local
market price differences, for example by indexing the CfD to a national average price12. This, in turn,
could increase investment risks, as the development of local prices could be hard for investors to
predict. It is therefore preferable to steer the deployment of capacity through a locational auction
mechanism, while providing a high degree of security to those investments that receive a CfD,
indexing said contract to the local price. In this way, operators could be assured to receive the strike

Locational investment signals should further be applied to investment mechanisms beyond renewable
auctions. Outside of Europe, system operators have already gathered experience with locational
capacity markets, as CAISO, PJM and Chile have introduced either a nodal or a zonal component to
the market clearing mechanism. Introducing similar elements in European capacity markets would
ensure that investments into firm capacity occur in the right locations, enabling a feasible
(re)dispatch. For hydrogen, the introduction appears to be a no-regret option, as a spatial correlation
between hydrogen production and renewable surpluses would lead to less stress on the grid (50Hertz
et al., 2023; Vom Scheidt et al., 2022). The introduction should further be evaluated in the context of
flexibility auctions, ensuring that storage and demand response are enabled in locations where they
can most effectively relieve bottlenecks. In this context, it has to be noted that an effective use of
flexibility for congestion management requires additionally price signals that reflect the local grid
conditions.


6.2 Bidding zone reconfiguration
Bidding zone splits are the instrument currently foreseen by the European legislation to increase the
geographical resolution at which the market is cleared. The BZR is conducted regularly and proposes
new bidding zone configurations that address structural congestion in the grid. The current iteration
of the BZR proposed to increase the number of European zones by four – still a far way to go from
the Multi Split scenario investigated in this study which relied on 101 market zones.

The current iteration further started in 2020, and presented final results in spring of 2025. This
process would have to be sped up to reach a configuration which is closer aligned with the physical
reality in the grid. In addition, more ambitious splits should be investigated to ensure that a system
is reached that is sufficiently granular to efficiently coordinate the dispatch of flexible resources.

To achieve a more efficient dispatch, it could further be reasonable to expand the criteria necessary
to warrant a split of bidding zones. While reducing structural congestion should undoubtedly remain
a major objective, the system could further benefit from smaller zones, as it would allow to activate
resources in a more targeted way, for example with the objective of relieving bottlenecks on cross-
border lines.
The main downside of a continuous splitting of bidding zones is the increased risk that such a strategy
would expose investors to. With new splits being implemented every couple of years, they would
always be exposed to the risk of finding themselves in a zone with unfavourable prices. (Maurer et
al., 2018) have therefore argued that a one-off reform of the market could be preferable to an
iterative redesign of bidding zones. Potentially, such a reform could be implemented through the BZR.
This would, however, require a longer-term outlook, investigating which bidding zone configurations
would be fit for a net-zero energy system. The outcome could well comprise of several hundred
market zones, and might resemble an LMP system, at least one of a comparably coarse resolution.


6.3 Locational marginal pricing
The benefits of an LMP implementation assessed in this case study are substantial, with 26-61 Bn.
EUR of annual savings, depending on how much grid expansion proves to be feasible. Even more
importantly, LMP operation was able to ensure a completely reliable operation of the system, even if
investments occurred at suboptimal locations. Zonal operation of the same systems led to high



                                                   52
volumes of load shedding in 2040. This highlights the importance of aligning market operations
closely with the physical reality, as previously noted as well by PSE and experts from Amprion (PSE,
2018; Sapp et al., 2024).

At the same time, an implementation of LMP would require substantial policy action, amounting to a
fundamental reform of the European market rules. While the current market rules do not inhibit a
transition towards LMP in principle (Antonopoulos et al., 2020), some elements may impede its
introduction. (Scott, 2024) compiled a comprehensive overview of policy measures that might be
necessary to successfully implement LMP in the European electricity market.

An introduction of LMP would likely warrant a simplification of bidding formats currently used by
EUPHEMIA, potentially transitioning to unit-based bidding. While portfolios could theoretically be
nominated as well at each location, a change towards unit-based bids would be reasonable: Such
portfolios would comprise of a much lower volume than today’s portfolio bids, thereby losing a key
advantage of the format. Instead, some portfolios would potentially consist only of one power plant.
Unit-based bidding could, however, provide additional advantages, for example facilitate market
surveillance. As regulators are clearly aware what kind of power plant is associated with which bid,
marginal costs can easier be estimated, allowing for more effective market power mitigation (Hogan
and Pope, 2017). Further, the differentiation between congestion management and balancing,
introduced as part of the fourth energy package, would need to be removed, since an LMP real-time
market solves congestion constraints in lockstep with balancing needs (Neuhoff et al., 2024).

Institutional changes appear further necessary for a successful implementation of LMP, as the
operation of the market is related to the system’s operation to a much higher degree. Accordingly,
power markets relying on LMP are often operated by an independent system operator (ISO). Creating
ISOs in Europe could require splitting the system’s operation from the operation of the transmission
network, and joining it with the operation of the market (Scott, 2024). (Neuhoff et al., 2024) however
argued that a more practical approach could be an increased cooperation, or potentially even mergers,
between TSOs and NEMOs.


6.4 Mitigating potential risk increases for renewables
There is an ongoing discussion to what degree more granular locational pricing increases risk for
renewable investments, erasing some or all benefits associated with reforms. These assessments
focus on the increase in hurdle rates for renewable investments due to higher risks during the
merchant tail phase – as well as on higher volume risks, since market periods with a renewable
generation surplus could occur more frequently in smaller zones or under LMP.

While our results suggest that investors would benefit financially in the Locational scenario during
the merchant tail phase, risks were identified only for the Multi Split scenario, representing a clear
disadvantage of this scenario. A straightforward way to address these risks would be to shorten the
merchant tail by offering CfDs for a longer period, if an ambitious bidding zone split scenario was
about to be pursued. Our results indicate that this would even benefit European consumers, as CfDs
result in net payments from generators to consumers across all target years and all scenarios.
Extending contract periods could therefore marry the desire of investors for safe revenues with lower
costs to consumers.

Volume risks, as identified by (Blyth et al., 2024) in a UK context, could further be addressed through
an adapted CfD design where necessary. (Schlecht et al., 2022) have previously proposed a contract
design under which renewable operators would receive a monthly payment, ensuring a high level of
revenue security. At the same time, they would be exposed to a zero strike price, effectively paying


                                                  53
back the expected market revenue. The payments would be settled based on the performance of a
virtual reference plant to ensure the asset is marketed efficiently on the wholesale market. This
reference could well be calibrated based on the same location of the real installation, referencing a
weather forecast picked by the renewable operator, as proposed by (Newbery, 2023). In this way,
volume risk would not be an issue, as the virtual reference would be exposed to the same market
conditions as the real one. As an added benefit, such a contract design would alleviate other
distortions that go hand in hand with conventional CfDs (Newbery, 2023; Schlecht et al., 2024).

7 Conclusions and policy recommendations
In this study, we have assessed three different market clearing resolutions in the context of the
European electricity market: The Status Quo, a Multi Split scenario with 101 zones, as well as a
locationally optimized scenario (Locational) with locational investment signals and locational
marginal pricing (LMP). The two scenarios relying on bidding zones (Status Quo and Multi Split) were
further assessed in combination with locational investment signals.

We have identified substantial reliability benefits in the context of the Locational scenario: While both,
Status Quo and Multi Split scenario led to system adequacy concerns, the Locational scenario was
always able to maintain a perfectly reliable supply. Locational investment signals were not sufficient
to solve this issue, indicating that these load shedding events were not primarily a result of
investment in wrong locations, but rather a result of a suboptimal operation of the system. This was
further confirmed by simulating an LMP operation of the Status Quo and Multi Split systems, which
always resulted in perfect reliability. LMP enables an effective integration of flexible assets, such as
storage and demand response, which, in turn, become important pillars of the system’s security. We
further note that the Multi Split scenario already improves the reliability of the system considerably,
leading to substantially lower volumes of load shedding.

This shows that markets with larger bidding zones are unable to provide the necessary operational
and investment signals to ensure system adequacy without out-of-the-market measures. Instead,
larger bidding zones require substantial amounts of excess capacity, the associated investment costs
of which are not covered by market prices. Maintaining the Status Quo bidding zone configuration
would therefore require creating large reserves outside the market over the next decades to ensure
a safe supply.

Due to lower capacity and fuel needs, the Locational scenario resulted in substantial economic
benefits. System cost savings in 2040 were estimated at 26-61 Bn. EUR annually in the Locational
scenario and at 10-37 Bn. EUR/a in the Multi Split scenario, an order of magnitude comparable to the
savings associated with the integration of European electricity markets. While wholesale prices
increase if congestion management measures are being internalized, a more efficient dispatch of
flexibility reduces the dependence on costly fuels. Simultaneously, this leads to less capacity being
needed to maintain a stable operation of the system. In zonal markets, on the other hand, storage
operation was found to regularly operate against the needs of the system, exacerbating both scarcity
and surplus situations. These findings align with those of the German TSO TenneT, who identified
similar shortcomings in a German context (TenneT, 2024).

Most of the identified cost savings are passed on to consumers, with consumer costs being reduced
by 24-58 Bn. EUR each year in the Locational scenario and by 9-35 Bn. EUR/a in the Multi Split
scenario. Considering the distribution of these benefits, we further find that the overwhelming part
of European consumers would benefit under both scenarios – in 62-90% of the cells that underpin
the model – while end-consumer cost increases beyond 5 EUR/MWh, compared to the Status Quo, are
extremely rare. Less than 1.2% of all cells saw an increase that exceeded this threshold in both
reform scenarios and under all sensitivities. More substantial increases occurred almost exclusively
in Northern Scandinavia and on the Western Balkans.
A substantial part of these benefits can be achieved by introducing locational investment signals.
Considering the Status Quo scenario, their introduction resulted in system cost savings between 12-
29 Bn. EUR/a in 2040. Their introduction can be considered as a no-regret option, and as a vital
element going forward, considering the large volume of power sector investment that is expected to
come on line within the next decades. If these investments take place in the wrong locations, they


                                                   55
could substantially increase the total cost of the system, while corrections would take a long time.
Locational investment signals should be implemented even if the spatial resolution of market clearing
is increased through bidding zone splits or an introduction of LMP, as previous research suggests that
short-term pricing might not suffice to incentivize efficient investment decisions (Brown et al., 2020).

Producers further tend to profit from more granular locational pricing, increasing their profits in most
scenarios, and by up to 7%. Internalizing congestion management measures increases the margins
that can be generated in the market, as they tend to increase wholesale prices, as well as arbitrage
opportunities.

Further, the Locational scenario results in stable or increased market values for renewable
technologies. This is especially relevant with regard to discussions whether there is a risk of a reduced
profitability during the merchant tail period, which could result in higher capital costs. This risk does
not appear to be substantial under the Locational scenario, given the high profitability of renewable
investments. Our analysis further suggests that substantial hurdle rate increases between 1.1% and
2.8% would be necessary to wipe out all system cost savings. The situation is somewhat different in
the Multi Split scenario, as market values of renewable generators are reduced in several cases. In
addition, the break-even hurdle rates were estimated to be between 0.4% and 1.7%. In both scenarios,
the lower break-even hurdle rate occurred, however, in the scenario with optimal grid expansion, while
the highest occurred when inner-country grid expansion was limited to 20%.

These circumstances convey that risk to renewable investments must be minimized to maximize the
benefits that can be achieved by more granular locational pricing. There appear to be, however,
policies which could alleviate these risks by taking over price- and volume risks altogether. Contract
durations of CfDs could be extended, to reduce the merchant tail period, and thereby the associated
risks. While we could not identify an increased risk in this period in the case of the Locational scenario,
this might be a necessary measure if a Multi Split scenario should be pursued. At the same time,
European consumers would likely benefit from longer contract durations: Our results suggest that
CfDs result in a net payment to consumers, lowering their energy bills in both, 2030 and 2040.
Adapting the design of those CfDs could further alleviate any volume risks, as discussed in section
6.4. Please note that while CfDs generate a benefit for consumers in both target years of our analysis,
it can, of course, not be ruled out that this balance reverses further down the road. After years of
substantial additional renewable investment, market values of renewables could decrease further,
leading to CfDs resulting in net payments from consumers to producers.

Given the large benefits that were observed in the Locational scenario – which were found to be in
the order of magnitude of the benefits associated with European electricity market integration – we
recommend the introduction of LMP and of locational investment signals. While this would imply a
substantial reform of the way that electricity is being traded in Europe, the benefits with regard to
system security and the system’s cost appears to rectify such a reform. On the other hand, it appears
hardly feasible to efficiently incorporate flexibility into the system in a market setting with large
bidding zones.


7.1 Policy roadmap
As a first step in this direction, we recommend to define LMP as a part of the target model, while
aiming for an introduction in the 2030s. This would give market participants sufficient time to prepare
for the reform, while allowing for policy measures that flank the introduction of LMP.

Accordingly, locational investment signals should be strengthened or introduced for all investment
mechanisms as soon as possible. The evaluation of locational impact as part of renewable auctions


                                                    56
should be mandatory in every country with congestion issues. A locational criterion should further be
introduced in forward capacity markets, hydrogen and flexibility auctions to ensure that the system
we build today is fit for a net zero future.

Trading hubs for forward trading, as proposed by (ACER, 2023a), should be established to detach
hedging activities from concrete bidding zone delineations. Further measures – such as unit-based
bidding – can be introduced in today’s setting, while ensuring an efficient bidding process in an LMP
system. For a comprehensive list of policy measures that can accompany the introduction of LMP in
a European setting, we refer the reader to (Scott, 2024).

Further, most benefits with regard to a 2030 horizon can be achieved with an ambitious bidding zone
split scenario. Implementing the DE5 split, which (ENTSO-E, 2025a) assessed to reduce European
redispatch by 50%, thus remains a no-regret option. In the next iteration of the BZR, more ambitious
splits could further be investigated – similar to the one presented in the Multi Split scenario – as these
could prepare the European power system for the 2030s, reducing the need for a swift introduction
of LMP, thereby allowing for a thorough implementation. At the same time, another BZR, triggering
substantial bidding zone reconfigurations, could potentially bind substantial resources and lead to
significant uncertainty if it was clear to market participants that LMP was about to be introduced only
a couple of years later. This is a trade-off that needs to be weighed carefully.
